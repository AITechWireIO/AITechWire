Ripple wins final OCC approval for national trust bank

Ripple's RNTB receives final OCC charter approval, enabling RLUSD under federal oversight after conditional OK in 2025. Completes major regulatory milestone for stablecoin and banking push.

AI TechWire · October 27, 2026 · 4 min read
Photo by Coinhako / Unsplash

Ripple's Federal Bank Charter Is the Institutional Crypto Moment Everyone Said Would Never Come

Ripple just crossed a threshold that most crypto firms only theorize about: a fully approved national trust bank operating under U.S. federal supervision. The OCC's final sign-off on Ripple National Trust Bank isn't a regulatory footnote — it's the architecture of a new financial institution, and it shifts the ground beneath every stablecoin issuer and institutional custody provider in the market.

What Happened

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, overseen by Comptroller Jonathan Gould, granted Ripple final approval to establish Ripple National Trust Bank (RNTB) on October 26, 2026. The process began when Ripple first applied for the banking license in July 2025. The OCC conditionally approved that application in December 2025 — alongside those of Circle, Paxos, Fidelity Digital Assets, and BitGo — before signing off fully this week.

Once operational, RNTB will manage the segregated reserves backing Ripple's RLUSD stablecoin, act as collateral trustee of those reserves for the benefit of RLUSD holders, and provide fiduciary digital asset custody services to enterprises. Crucially, RLUSD will continue to be issued by Standard Custody & Trust Company, Ripple's limited purpose trust company regulated by the New York Department of Financial Services. The result is a dual-layer oversight structure: state and federal regulators now both have eyes on different parts of the RLUSD apparatus.

RLUSD launched in December 2024 and has since grown to a market capitalization of approximately $2.6 billion, according to CoinGecko, backed by U.S. dollar deposits, U.S. government securities, and other cash-like assets. BNY serves as the stablecoin's primary reserve custodian. The stablecoin is used across Ripple's payments network and as collateral within Ripple Prime, the company's brokerage platform.

Why It Matters: The Regulated Rails Advantage

Institutional money doesn't move on enthusiasm — it moves on legal certainty. A federally chartered national trust bank gives Ripple something that a state license alone cannot: a single recognized credential that works across all fifty states, supervised directly by the OCC. For a company whose entire commercial pitch centers on cross-border payments and institutional crypto infrastructure, that matters enormously.

The dual oversight structure is equally significant. RLUSD now sits at the intersection of NYDFS regulation through Standard Custody & Trust Company and federal supervision through RNTB. For a corporate treasury team, a fund administrator, or a payments desk evaluating stablecoin counterparty risk, that layered oversight is a feature, not a complication. It makes RLUSD a meaningfully harder asset to dismiss in a compliance review than a stablecoin operating under a single, state-level framework.

The timing is no accident. Ripple confirmed it will keep working with the OCC, NYDFS, and other regulators as the bank begins operations and as the U.S. implements the GENIUS Act's stablecoin framework. That positions RNTB not just as a present-tense institution but as infrastructure built for whatever regulatory architecture Congress finalizes. Ripple is not waiting for the rules to settle — it is installing itself inside the process.

On X, Garlinghouse framed it precisely: "The revolution isn't happening outside the system. It's happening from within it." That line isn't rhetoric. It is the business model.

The Fight That Isn't Over

Not everyone is welcoming Ripple into the banking system. Senator Elizabeth Warren has argued in a letter to Comptroller Gould that the OCC's crypto charter approvals — including Ripple's — violated the National Bank Act. Her language was direct: "These companies are effectively crypto banks that want to evade the fundamental safeguards and obligations that come with being a bank."

That political pressure has materialized into litigation. Earlier this month, the Independent Community Bankers of America sued the OCC in federal court, arguing that the charters have become what it called "a side door into the banking system for crypto firms" that sidestep capital requirements, consolidated supervision, and FDIC insurance obligations that traditional banks must carry. The Digital Chamber, the crypto industry trade group, responded by urging the OCC to defend the charters and continue developing supervisory standards for trust banks.

The lawsuit is the story within the story. If a federal court rules against the OCC's authority to grant these charters, Ripple's RNTB approval — and those of Circle, Paxos, Fidelity Digital Assets, and BitGo — become legally precarious overnight. Ripple has not commented publicly on the litigation, but the company's commitment to ongoing regulatory engagement suggests it is treating the legal challenge as a known variable, not a surprise.

Resolution: A Foundation, Not a Finish Line

Ripple has spent more than a decade arguing that regulated infrastructure is the only durable path into institutional finance. RNTB is the fullest expression of that thesis yet — a federally supervised entity with a mandate covering reserve management, collateral trusteeship, and digital asset custody. The $2.6 billion RLUSD market cap it supports is already material. With federal oversight now layered on top of state regulation, RLUSD has a credibility stack that most stablecoins cannot replicate quickly.

What happens next depends on two external variables Ripple cannot control: the outcome of the ICBA's federal lawsuit against the OCC, and the shape of the final GENIUS Act stablecoin framework. Both will define how wide the lane actually is for RNTB's institutional ambitions. For now, Ripple holds the charter. The open question is whether the courts let it keep it.

What It Means

  • RLUSD gains a credibility edge: Dual state and federal oversight makes the stablecoin more defensible in institutional compliance reviews — this likely accelerates adoption in payments and capital markets use cases where counterparty scrutiny is highest.
  • Competitors face a structural gap: Circle, Paxos, Fidelity Digital Assets, and BitGo received conditional OCC approval alongside Ripple in December 2025; Ripple's final approval sets a benchmark and adds pressure on peers to complete their own charter processes.
  • The ICBA lawsuit is the key risk to watch: A court ruling against OCC authority to grant crypto trust charters would threaten not just RNTB but the entire cohort of conditionally approved firms — the legal outcome matters as much as the regulatory one.
  • GENIUS Act implementation is now Ripple's next regulatory arena: With RNTB operational, Ripple is positioned to engage directly in shaping how the new federal stablecoin framework applies to federally chartered trust banks — an inside-the-tent advantage that pure state-regulated issuers won't have.
