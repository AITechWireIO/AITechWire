Fed's Williams Signals More Rate Hikes Needed on Inflation

NY Fed President Williams said inflation remains too high with work to do, pricing in Dec hike and further tightening. Matters as it shifts rate expectations, impacts bonds, equities, and risk assets ahead of Dec FOMC.

AI TechWire · 25, 2026 · 4 min read
Photo by Paul ACat / Unsplash

Williams Puts Consecutive Rate Hikes on the Table — and Markets Are Listening

New York Fed President John C. Williams just made the Fed's next move about as close to a certainty as central bank language ever gets, and then raised the stakes further. Inflation is "too high," back-to-back hikes are firmly on the table, and the path back to 2% now stretches all the way to 2028.

What Happened: A Clear Signal Wrapped in Careful Language

Speaking Monday at the College of Mount Saint Vincent in New York, Williams delivered what markets needed little help decoding. "Inflation is too high," he said flatly, adding that "we still have work to do to return inflation to our 2% target in a timely and sustained way." He flagged the December 8–9 Federal Open Market Committee meeting as a live event for another rate increase, while pointedly refusing to cap how far or how fast tightening could go.

When reporters pressed him on timing, Williams was deliberate. "I don't have a particular timing in mind," he said — but followed that immediately with the line that sent rate futures lurching: "I would not at all rule out raising rates at consecutive meetings if needed." That is Fed-speak for a world where the January meeting is not a pause; it is another hike.

Source: John C. Williams, NY Fed President - "We must take actions over time that will ensure that we get inflation back to our 2 per cent goal in a timely manner and on a sustained basis. There is still work to do to get that done." link

Markets moved accordingly. By Monday evening, the CME Group's FedWatch tool placed high probability on a 25-basis-point December hike, with a small portion wagering on a larger move. Looking further out, markets priced in the likelihood of the benchmark rate rising by January, implying two consecutive hikes before winter is out.

Why It Matters: The Inflation Drivers Are Not Going Away

Williams was careful to distinguish between inflation's sources, and that distinction carries real weight for portfolio positioning. The effects of tariffs on prices have "largely faded," he said — but elevated energy prices tied to the Middle East conflict and a supply-demand imbalance in semiconductors driven by AI infrastructure buildout are actively fueling price pressure. His base case: inflation ends 2026 at 3.5%, moderates to just above 2% next year, and only reaches the Fed's target in 2028.

That two-year glide path is the number that should concentrate minds in fixed income and equity strategy alike. It tells bond desks that the Fed is not close to pivoting, and it tells equity analysts that the discount rate stays restrictive through at least mid-cycle. Williams did note that "although the effects of tariffs, geopolitical conflicts, and the AI surge are showing up in prices of certain categories of goods, there is little evidence of these spilling over significantly into broader and more persistent inflation" — a caveat that keeps the door cracked for a less aggressive path, but does not change the direction of travel. He also projected the economy growing solidly in both 2026 and 2027, which removes the recession-imminent argument from the dovish corner of the debate for now.

"I would not at all rule out raising rates at consecutive meetings if needed."

— John C. Williams, President, Federal Reserve Bank of New York

For risk assets, the inflation drivers Williams cited carry sector-specific implications. Energy equities get a tailwind from the conflict premium embedded in oil prices, but cyclical stocks face the dual headwind of elevated yields and a dollar that Citadel Securities notes is rising in a pattern reminiscent of late 2018. At that time, the same combination — a hawkish Fed holding Treasury yields high, rising dollar, cyclical underperformance — preceded a roughly 20% decline in the S&P 500 from its September high into December.

The Counter-Case: 50 Basis Points and the Ackman Warning

Williams is not operating in a vacuum, and the debate inside the Fed is sharper than his measured tone suggests. Fed Governor Christopher Waller has argued for a 50-basis-point move at the December meeting, citing strong inflationary pressure — a position Williams explicitly pushed back on, saying the current situation does not call for "dramatic action." The internal disagreement matters because it sets the outer boundary of what a hawkish surprise at December's FOMC could actually look like. Markets are pricing low odds on 50 basis points; Waller's voice keeps that tail risk alive.

Outside the Fed, the criticism runs in the opposite direction. Billionaire investor Bill Ackman posted on X that the Fed is "about to make another mistake" and criticized its 2% inflation target as out of step with reality. Meanwhile, Citadel Securities drew the late-2018 comparison explicitly, noting that firm domestic demand is again masking a deteriorating growth outlook — and that the current conflict with Iran has compounded the pressure in ways that did not exist in 2018. In that prior episode, then-Governor Kevin Warsh — now Chair of the Board of Governors — called publicly for a halt to tightening. That history adds an uncomfortable subtext to the current moment: the person who once urged the Fed to stop is now the one running it, as inflation remains more than 150 basis points above target.

Resolution: Rates Are Going Higher, the Only Question Is How Fast

Williams drew a clear line between what he will and will not support. He favors continued tightening, resists a single large step, and refuses to rule out consecutive hikes. That framing — gradual but relentless — is the operative monetary policy posture heading into December. The Fed last raised rates in September; a December move would resume the tightening cycle after a one-meeting hold, and Williams's comments make clear that the pause was data-dependent, not a policy signal of any kind.

The open question is whether two consecutive 25-basis-point hikes get inflation moving toward target fast enough to satisfy a Fed that has watched prices sit above 2% for more than five years. Williams's own forecast — 3.5% at year-end, slightly above 2% next year, on target only in 2028 — suggests the answer is probably not, which means the pressure for more action will not dissipate after January regardless of what markets price today.

What It Means

  • Bond investors should treat Williams's remarks as confirmation that Treasury yields stay elevated through at least early 2027; a higher rate floor by January is now the market consensus, and duration risk remains asymmetric to the downside.
  • Equity portfolios face a persistent headwind: Citadel Securities' late-2018 analogy — rising dollar, elevated yields, cyclical underperformance — is the nearest historical template, and this time geopolitical risk in energy markets adds an inflation floor that did not exist then.
  • Energy and AI infrastructure are the two inflation drivers Williams named explicitly; this likely means those sectors remain focal points for both inflationary surprise and policy response in the months ahead.
  • Watch the January FOMC: with markets already pricing a high chance of back-to-back hikes, any softening in incoming data between now and then becomes the single most important variable for whether Williams's "not ruling it out" becomes an actual vote.
