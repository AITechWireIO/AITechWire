Strong Oct jobs data boosts Dec Fed hike odds to 80%+

Data revives inflation concerns over AI slowdown fears.

AI TechWire · November 08, 2026 · 4 min read
Photo by Brecht Corbeel / Unsplash

The labor market just made the Federal Reserve's December decision a lot harder to avoid. October's blowout payrolls number — nearly triple what Wall Street expected — has reignited inflation fears, pushed rate-hike odds sharply higher, and put risk assets from crypto to tech stocks squarely in the crosshairs of a Fed chair with no apparent interest in backing down.

What Just Happened

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported Friday that U.S. employers added 165,000 nonfarm payroll jobs in October, crushing the Dow Jones consensus estimate of 59,000. The unemployment rate dropped to 4.1%, beating forecasts of 4.2%. Average hourly earnings climbed 0.4% for the month and 3.1% year-over-year, both ahead of expectations.

The revisions told almost as important a story as the headline. August's job gain was revised up 53,000 to 186,000. September was lifted by 31,000 to 60,000 — flipping from an initial 29,000 print that had given the Fed political cover to hold rates in October. Combined, those two months added 84,000 jobs the FOMC did not have in front of it at its October 27–28 meeting. A labor market that looked like it was finally cooling now looks like it never stopped running.

Professional and business services led all sectors with 70,000 jobs added. Health care contributed 42,000, transportation and warehousing 25,000, and manufacturing 19,000. The Fed had held its benchmark rate at 3.75% to 4% at the October meeting, but futures markets moved fast after Friday's release. CME Group's FedWatch tool placed the probability of a quarter-point December hike at roughly 77%, up approximately 10 points in a single session, with the December 8–9 FOMC meeting now the last scheduled decision of 2026 and the only one carrying a fresh Summary of Economic Projections.

"Today's jobs report is a strong rebuttal to fears that the economy is heading into a meaningful slowdown. Payrolls growth surprised firmly to the upside, unemployment fell back to 4.1%, and job creation remains broad-based, pointing to an economy that is still absorbing labor comfortably rather than one beginning to buckle under tighter financial conditions."

- Seema Shah, Chief Global Strategist, Principal Asset Management

Why It Matters: Inflation Over AI

Fed Chair Kevin Warsh has repeatedly kept the door open to further rate hikes while inflation runs above the central bank's 2% target. The October data delivers him both the cover and the pressure to walk through it. At the October meeting, Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack had already voted to raise rates rather than hold — dissents that now look prescient rather than hawkish outliers.

The inflation math is uncomfortable. Average hourly earnings grew 3.1% annually, but consumer prices rose through September. The average worker's pay is still losing ground in real terms, even as the headline labor market looks robust. That gap — strong hiring, negative real wages — is precisely the kind of stagflationary signal that complicates the Fed's dual mandate and limits its ability to pivot toward growth.

Source: Addison Maier, Portfolio Manager, Janus Henderson Investors - "While this release points towards a December hike which is ~75% priced, on a forward-looking basis, especially in light of the volatility this week, what will matter most for the Fed is inflation and the evolving outlook for the AI buildout." link

The bond market confirmed the pivot. The 10-year Treasury yield rose more than 3 basis points to roughly 4.87%. The 30-year climbed to around 5.28%. The 2-year note — the instrument most sensitive to near-term Fed expectations — jumped nearly 5 basis points to 4.52%. For mortgage borrowers, the Mortgage Bankers Association's Mike Fratantoni was unequivocal: a resilient job market combined with still-elevated inflation is likely to push the FOMC to hike in December, with the MBA anticipating another increase in Q1 2027 and 30-year mortgage rates above 7% through all of next year.

Source: Mike Fratantoni, Chief Economist, Mortgage Bankers Association - "A resilient job market, combined with inflation that is still too high, is likely to lead the FOMC to hike rates in December. MBA anticipates another hike as well in the first quarter of 2027." link

The Counter-Case: Cracks Beneath the Surface

Not every analyst is reading the same report. Fratantoni pointed to a complication that the headline unemployment rate obscures: the U-6 measure — which captures people working part-time involuntarily alongside those loosely attached to the labor force — rose in September. Residential construction shed more than 15,000 jobs in October, a direct consequence of the rate environment depressing purchase demand. And the median and average duration of unemployment spells are increasing, suggesting that workers who lose jobs are finding it harder to get back in.

Chris Zaccarelli, chief investment officer at Northlight Asset Management, made the case for resilience, citing GDP growth of 3.4% and corporate earnings up more than 25% year-over-year. But even his argument carried a warning: "Trees don't grow to the sky and of course there is a limit to how high interest rates can go before something breaks." The open question is not whether the economy is strong today — it clearly is — but whether a December hike combined with expectations for further tightening in early 2027 begins to find that limit.

Source: Jeff Schulze, Head Investment Strategist, Franklin Templeton Institute - "Lower unemployment in October gives the Fed more license to be hawkish as it leaves the committee free to continue placing the majority of its focus on restoring price stability." link

Resolution: December Is Live, and Markets Know It

Expectations for 2027 tightening had softened earlier in the week following midterm elections that handed Democrats control of both chambers of Congress, and a Thursday selloff in technology shares had pushed Nasdaq into correction territory. Friday's jobs print reversed that narrative in a single session. The rate-hike trade is back on. The prime rate, which has held steady since September, moves only if the Fed moves December 9. Futures now say the odds of that happening are close to 80%.

For risk assets — particularly the AI-adjacent tech stocks that drove this year's gains and the crypto markets that tend to reprice fastest when dollar rates rise — the calculus has shifted. Higher-for-longer is no longer a tail risk. It is the base case priced by the bond market, confirmed by the jobs data, and left deliberately open by the chair of the Federal Reserve.

What It Means

  • December hike is near-certain: CME FedWatch put the probability at roughly 77% immediately after the release, with the MBA forecasting a follow-on hike in Q1 2027 — this likely means two more rate increases before the Fed reassesses.
  • Mortgage borrowers face a long wait: With 30-year rates projected above 7% through 2027 and residential construction already shedding jobs, the housing market's rate relief is not arriving soon.
  • Tech and AI stocks carry elevated rate risk: A Thursday Nasdaq correction preceded Friday's data; a confirmed December hike removes the soft-landing narrative that has supported stretched valuations in AI-linked equities.
  • Watch the U-6 and real wages: The headline is strong, but underemployment rising and real hourly earnings still negative signal fragility beneath the surface — the data point to watch heading into the FOMC's December projections.
