Lyn Alden Bullish on Brazilian Stocks Long-Term

Lyn Alden noted buying Brazilian equities lower under Bolsonaro and remaining constructive through Lula. Highlights macro views on emerging markets resilient to political shifts.

AI TechWire · October 26, 2026 · 4 min read
Photo by Marcus Reubenstein / Unsplash

Lyn Alden has been building a constructive case on Brazilian equities through political cycles that would have rattled most macro investors — and the country's just delivered another one. With Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva winning a historic fourth term as president on October 25, 2026, defeating Flávio Bolsonaro in a presidential runoff, the emerging market thesis Alden has articulated for years faces its next stress test. Her argument, consistent across administrations, is that the macro floor under Brazilian equities is more durable than any single government — and the latest election result is, in her framing, just another chapter in a longer story.

The Political Backdrop

Brazil's October 25 runoff delivered a result that would have been unthinkable a decade ago: Lula, whose political career spans decades and whose fourth term crowns one of Latin America's most consequential democratic careers, beat Flávio Bolsonaro — son of Jair Bolsonaro, the former president now serving a 27-year sentence under house arrest for coup plotting. The elder Bolsonaro's legal jeopardy effectively handed the opposition mantle to his son, but it wasn't enough.

For investors positioned in Brazilian equities, the immediate question is whether the Lula win reshuffles the deck on valuations, fiscal policy risk, and currency. Flávio Bolsonaro has already refused to recognize the result, according to Reuters, injecting a degree of political noise into the post-election market open. That friction is real, and it matters for short-term positioning. But Alden's framework specifically discounts the noise in favor of the structural signal.

Her approach — buying Brazilian equities at lower prices during the Bolsonaro years and remaining constructive through the Lula administration — treats political risk as priced-in volatility rather than thesis-ending disruption. The core insight is that emerging market equities, particularly in commodity-linked economies, are driven by dollar cycles, commodity price regimes, and global liquidity conditions to a degree that domestic politics rarely overrides for long.

Why the Macro Floor Holds

The global macro backdrop Alden's thesis sits inside is, right now, genuinely complicated. U.S. crude is trading near $101 a barrel, with Brent slipping to around $110, according to CoinDesk market data. That's still an elevated commodity environment — one that historically flatters Brazil's export revenues from oil and agricultural goods. At the same time, oil retreated on Monday as Qatari mediators arrived in Tehran and geopolitical escalation risk briefly receded, demonstrating just how fast commodity tailwinds can shift.

The Federal Reserve, now chaired by Kevin Warsh — who took office in May 2026 — is set to decide on rates this Wednesday. The CME FedWatch tool shows a 90% chance of no change, with around an 80% chance of another increase in December. That means the dollar rate environment remains tight, which has historically created headwinds for emerging market currencies and capital flows. The Brazilian real is not immune to that dynamic. A December Fed hike, if it materializes, would tighten the screws on EM positioning broadly.

And yet the counter-argument to rate-driven pessimism on EM is exactly what Alden has been making: you buy emerging market equities when they're cheap relative to the commodity and dollar cycle, not when the narrative is cleanest. Political transition moments — messy, contested, uncertain — are often where that discount is widest. Flávio Bolsonaro's refusal to concede is precisely the kind of headline that can suppress valuations even when the underlying macro story hasn't changed. That's the window Alden's framework is built to exploit.

The Strongest Counter-View

The bear case on Brazil right now isn't primarily political — it's monetary and global. S&P 500 futures rose around 0.3% after Friday's selloff, suggesting U.S. equity markets are still absorbing their own turbulence. Global shares are cautious ahead of what Reuters describes as a "formidable" week for risk, with central bank meetings and corporate earnings simultaneously testing confidence in the AI-driven growth narrative. Capital that might otherwise rotate into EM equities is sitting on its hands.

Bitcoin, trading near $80,400 on Monday, is itself a proxy for global risk appetite and dollar liquidity conditions. Spot bitcoin ETF flows logged $772 million in two-day outflows heading into the week, with $138 million exiting on Friday alone. Arthur Hayes disclosed over the weekend that his Maelstrom fund sold its entire directional book — including 6,400 ether — citing macro and geopolitical risk. That kind of institutional risk-off posture doesn't stay confined to crypto. It bleeds into EM equity sentiment, into currency positioning, into the willingness to hold reais-denominated assets through a contested election.

The open question is whether a December Fed rate hike, combined with sustained political noise out of Brasília, constitutes a temporary dip in an otherwise constructive EM cycle — or the beginning of a more sustained outflow period. Alden's answer, based on her historical positioning, has been consistent: the dip is the opportunity. But the timing of when that opportunity resolves is never clean.

What it means

  • Brazil's re-election of Lula — contested by Flávio Bolsonaro — creates short-term headline risk that this likely means discounted entry points for investors who share Alden's structural EM thesis and can tolerate political volatility.
  • The Fed's Wednesday decision, with a 90% probability of no change per CME FedWatch, removes one near-term risk; a December hike at 80% implied probability is the more meaningful EM headwind to watch.
  • Commodity prices — U.S. crude near $101, Brent near $110 — remain supportive of Brazil's macro export story, but the Monday pullback on geopolitical de-escalation signals how quickly that tailwind can soften.
  • Investors calibrating EM exposure should watch the Bolsonaro camp's post-election posture and any fiscal policy signals from a fourth Lula term; those are the variables most likely to either compress or expand the discount that long-term EM buyers depend on.
