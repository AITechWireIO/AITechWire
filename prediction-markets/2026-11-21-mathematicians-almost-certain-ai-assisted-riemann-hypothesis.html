Mathematicians almost certain AI-assisted Riemann hypothesis proof is real

Matters as it flips narrative from AI skepticism to rapid scientific breakthroughs.

AI TechWire · November 21, 2026 · 4 min read
Photo by Jan Demiralp / Unsplash

The most famous unsolved problem in mathematics appears to be solved — and artificial intelligence did it. Independent mathematicians are now almost certain that Levent Alpöge, a mathematician and employee at Anthropic, has proven the Riemann hypothesis using the company's Claude AI model, flipping the dominant narrative about AI from cautious skepticism to something far more disruptive: a machine-assisted conquest of science itself.

What Happened

On Friday, Alpöge posted a statement on his website claiming he had cracked the Riemann hypothesis — first proposed in 1859 by German mathematician Bernhard Riemann — which holds that the locations of prime numbers along the number line, seemingly random, are actually governed by a deep and meaningful order. The problem is one of the Clay Mathematics Institute's Millennium Prize Problems, carrying a $1 million bounty, and has long been considered so intractable that most top experts spent little time actually working on it.

Rather than publishing a traditional human-readable paper, Alpöge released his proof coded in Lean, a programming language used by mathematicians to mechanically verify proofs as a sequence of logical steps. If Lean code compiles without error, it is widely regarded as confirmation that every step in the argument is logically sound. Alpöge noted that a human-readable version ran to more than 250,000 pages — a scale that makes traditional peer review nearly meaningless as a first-pass check.

The critical development came quickly. Andrew Sutherland, a mathematician at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, independently compiled Alpöge's Lean proof without issues.

Source: Andrew Sutherland, MIT — "The Lean proof has been compiled and independently rechecked by the Lean kernel with only the three standard axioms. Honestly the chance that anything is fishy here is approximately zero." Scientific American

Alpöge also claims the proof extends to the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis (GRH) — a far broader statement that underpins vast regions of modern mathematics and on which hundreds of conditional theorems depend.

Why It Matters

The Riemann hypothesis is not merely a hard problem — it is the load-bearing wall of number theory. Proving it, and its generalization, instantly converts a large body of conditional mathematical results into established theorems. The cascade effect is difficult to overstate.

"If the proof is correct and understood, this completes a dictionary between different branches of math where the connections were only partial — something like a Rosetta Stone."

- Hector Pasten, Pontifical Catholic University of Chile

Carlo Pagano, a professor at Concordia University in Quebec and visiting researcher at Google DeepMind, went further, suggesting that even the methods behind the proof could matter as much as the result itself.

Source: Carlo Pagano, Concordia University / Google DeepMind — "If true, even from a first high level scan of the summary of the proposed argument, it is very plausible to me that the new ideas in the proof will open up brand new avenues of thinking in number theory and have an immense impact beside the obviously extremely influential result in itself." Scientific American

James McKee at Royal Holloway University of London captured the dual mood inside the mathematics community with unusual frankness: "It is both a wonderful time and a terrible time to be a research mathematician." For the AI industry, there is no such ambivalence. This result — if it holds — is a proof of concept at civilizational scale. It arrives on the heels of a year in which AI's mathematical capabilities, as one source put it, "ascended from unreliable college student to a superhuman force, leaving no part of the discipline undisrupted."

This is also not Alpöge's first headline of 2026. In July, he announced that the Jacobian conjecture — which mathematicians had spent decades trying to prove true — is actually false, producing a 216-character counterexample. He and Tristan Buckmaster at New York University also announced they had cracked three significant problems considered stepping stones to the Navier-Stokes problem. The Riemann proof is the capstone of a remarkable run, and it was built with Claude.

The Open Question

The proof is not yet fully ratified. One remaining step is what mathematicians call a "comparator run" — a check confirming that the Lean code does not redefine any mathematical symbols or exploit unknown bugs in Lean's official kernel. Kevin Buzzard of Imperial College London, one of the leading voices in the Lean community, has laid out the goalposts precisely.

Source: Kevin Buzzard, Imperial College London — "We still await a successful comparator run, which will check that (a) the code does not do anything nefarious like redefine the meaning of any of the symbols in the statement and (b) (assuming it's run in paranoid mode) the code compiles with several independent kernels (so the code is not just exploiting an as yet unknown bug in Lean's official kernel)." Scientific American

That comparator run typically takes a matter of hours. The logic of the proof also still needs human experts to assess the thrust of the argument — not just whether it compiles, but whether it truly addresses the Riemann hypothesis and not some subtly adjacent statement. The odds of a problem emerging are low by any expert's measure, but the standard for a 167-year-old Millennium Prize problem is rightly exacting.

Resolution

The story resolves in one direction regardless of whether the comparator run turns up a minor technical defect: AI-assisted formal mathematics has arrived as a credible force at the frontier of human knowledge. Alpöge's statement was elegiac in tone — "I believe it is one dawn across science of something far greater" — and the broader mathematical community seems to agree that whatever the final status of this specific proof, the era it announces is real and irreversible. Anthropic's August announcement had stopped short of a full proof; this claim does not. And this time, independent compilation has already occurred.

What It Means

  • Anthropic's Claude is now directly credited in what could become the most significant mathematical proof in modern history, a reputational and commercial asset that rivals any benchmark result the AI industry has produced.
  • If the GRH claim holds, hundreds of conditional mathematical theorems automatically become established facts — the downstream scientific and cryptographic implications will take years to fully map.
  • Watch for the comparator run result: a clean pass converts near-certainty into formal acceptance and likely triggers the Clay Mathematics Institute's $1 million prize process.
  • This likely accelerates institutional investment in AI-assisted formal proof systems across academia and industry — the question of whether AI belongs in research mathematics has effectively been answered.
