@saylor (Michael Saylor) weighing in: With CLARITY stalled, I expect the SEC, CFTC, and Treasury to advance rules under existing law, banks to expand Bitcoin custody and loans against it, and more capital to favor Bitcoin and digital cred

AI TechWire · September 19, 2026 · 5 min read
Photo by Brett Jordan / Unsplash

The CLARITY Act is dead — at least for now — and Washington's financial regulators aren't waiting around for Congress to get its act together. With the Senate failing to advance the landmark crypto legislation, the SEC and CFTC have shifted into independent rulemaking mode, and market observers like Strategy Inc. Executive Chairman Michael Saylor are already reading the tea leaves on what comes next for Bitcoin and digital assets.

CFTC Moves First, Details Still Murky

The day after the Senate's CLARITY Act stumble, the Commodity Futures Trading Commission wasted no time signaling it would act unilaterally. The CFTC submitted a new crypto market proposal to the White House Office of Management and Budget for review — a significant procedural step that sets the regulatory machinery in motion even as the substance of the draft remains largely opaque.

Details were not disclosed. It is unclear which crypto assets the proposal covers, what exchanges would need to do to qualify, what restrictions would apply, or how far the CFTC believes its existing authority actually extends. The draft will return to the CFTC for a vote and public comment once OMB completes its review, and would then require another vote to become effective.

CFTC Chair Mike Selig — sworn in as a Trump appointee and currently the only sitting commissioner amid four vacancies — made his position plain in a post on X following the Senate vote.

Source: CFTC Chair Mike Selig - "The CFTC is locked in and ready to ship its rules for the new frontier of finance." CoinDesk

That's a pointed statement from a regulator operating almost entirely alone. Four vacancies on a five-seat commission is an unusual pressure environment to be issuing market-structure rules from — but Selig appears undeterred.

The SEC Plays Its Own Hand

The Securities and Exchange Commission, led by Chair Paul Atkins, didn't sit on the sidelines either. On the same day the CFTC submitted its proposal to the OMB, the SEC issued an "innovation exemption" — a five-year conditional pathway for qualifying platforms to offer on-chain trading of certain tokenized stocks without first registering as securities exchanges.

The move is striking in its ambition. Rather than waiting for Congress to redraw jurisdictional lines between the SEC and CFTC — which was precisely what the CLARITY Act aimed to accomplish — the SEC is carving out regulatory space for tokenized equity markets under its existing authority. Both agencies have publicly committed to working together toward clearer digital asset rules despite the legislative vacuum.

"The CFTC is locked in and ready to ship its rules for the new frontier of finance."

- CFTC Chair Mike Selig

Also on Friday, the CFTC issued a no-action letter giving certain passive software providers — including some crypto wallet interfaces — a mechanism to connect users with regulated derivatives markets without registering as introducing brokers. Providers operating under the relief can market specific contracts and receive transaction-based fees, but face hard limits: they cannot hold customer assets, generate buy or sell signals, or control how orders are routed or executed. The relief comes bundled with requirements for risk disclosures, recordkeeping, and compliance with marketing rules, and remains in place until the CFTC adopts formal rules or guidance for software developers.

Taken together, the SEC's tokenized-stock exemption and the CFTC's no-action letter sketch the rough outlines of a parallel regulatory architecture — one being built agency by agency, exemption by exemption, in the absence of a comprehensive congressional framework.

Saylor's Signal and What It Means for Capital Flows

Into this environment stepped Michael Saylor, Executive Chairman and Chairman of the Board at Strategy Inc. — the Bitcoin-focused firm formerly known as MicroStrategy. Saylor, who has made Strategy Inc. into one of the most prominent institutional Bitcoin holders in the world, is already forecasting how the regulatory vacuum gets filled and where capital flows as a result.

His thesis, expressed publicly following the CLARITY Act's collapse: expect the SEC, CFTC, and Treasury — headed by Secretary Scott Bessent — to advance rules under their existing legal authority. He anticipates banks expanding Bitcoin custody services and offering loans collateralized by Bitcoin, with broader capital allocation increasingly favoring Bitcoin and digital credit instruments.

That read aligns directly with what the CFTC and SEC have already done in just the 48 hours since the Senate vote. The agencies are not pausing; they are accelerating. The no-action relief for wallet interfaces, in particular, is the kind of incremental clarification that lowers friction for institutional players without requiring a single vote from Capitol Hill.

The Verdict: Regulatory Patchwork, Not Paralysis

The failure of the CLARITY Act was supposed to introduce uncertainty into digital asset markets. Instead, it appears to have activated a regulatory sprint. The CFTC filing with OMB, the SEC's tokenized-stock exemption, and the no-action letter for software providers all landed within a 48-hour window — a pace that suggests both agencies had contingency plans drafted and ready to deploy.

What emerges from that sprint won't be the clean, unified framework the CLARITY Act promised. It will be a patchwork — jurisdictionally contested, open to legal challenge, and full of the ambiguity that comes when agencies act at the edge of their authority. But for an industry that spent years under a regulator openly hostile to digital assets, ambiguous forward motion is a significant improvement over enforcement-only posture.

Saylor is betting that capital follows clarity, even partial clarity. Based on what the CFTC and SEC did this week, he may be right sooner than Congress expected.
